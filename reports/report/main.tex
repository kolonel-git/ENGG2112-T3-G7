	\begin{center}
	\subsection*{Executive Summary}
	\end{center}

	\noindent Unplanned stops of wind turbines are expensive: they halt energy production, often need a crew and parts at short notice, and are usually noticed only after the turbine has already stopped. This project asks whether the data a turbine already records every ten minutes can warn of a coming fault or long stoppage in time to plan around it, and whether such a warning can be explained well enough for a maintenance engineer to trust it.

	We used the open Hill of Towie dataset (21 Siemens SWT-2.3-VS-82 turbines in Scotland, January 2016 to April 2026). Because the alarm log contains many routine events, we defined a failure as a \emph{fault episode}, in two independent ways: days with at least four hours of recorded fault time (target A), and at least three hours with the rotor stopped despite enough wind (target B). For every turbine-day we predicted whether a new episode would start in the next 30 days. We compared simple rules, a normal-behaviour model, logistic regression and class-weighted gradient boosting (LightGBM), trained on 2016--2018 and 2020, tuned on 2021--2022 and tested once on 2023 to April 2026, and we always compared each model with a random score, because about half of all days are positive.

	Our main finding is a cautious one. \textbf{On the test years no model could be distinguished from the random score}: every 95\% interval for the area under the precision--recall curve (PR-AUC) contains the random-score value. The model that looked best on the tuning years (a per-turbine z-score of temperatures) fell back to the random level on the test years, and the alert threshold chosen on the tuning years did not hold a fixed false-alarm rate on the test years. A model that learns the normal behaviour of the turbine temperatures gave a weak health indicator that drifts between years. Leaving a turbine out of training, or removing a group of sensors, changed the validation results very little, and the explanations were stable across turbines but describe a model with no demonstrated skill. We therefore do not recommend deploying any of these models; they should be used, at most, as an input to human planning, Exploratory follow-up runs on the tuning years only, with drift-robust features, raised some validation scores but not beyond the random-score margin of error; they are leads for future work and were not tested on the test years. The next steps are listed in Section~\ref{sec:disc}.

	\section{Background and Motivation}\label{sec:intro}

	Operation and maintenance (O\&M) typically accounts for 20--25\% of the levelised cost of energy (LCOE) of wind power, and 11--30\% for onshore projects \cite{irena2012}. Much of this comes from \emph{unscheduled} maintenance, where a component fails without warning and the turbine stays idle until technicians and parts arrive \cite{carroll2016}. Shifting from reactive or fixed-interval maintenance to \emph{predictive} maintenance, intervening when the data indicate that a failure is approaching, is one of the most direct ways to reduce this cost.

	Every utility-scale turbine already logs data through its \emph{Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition} (SCADA) system, typically as 10-minute statistics of power, speeds, wind, pitch and dozens of component temperatures, plus an alarm log. SCADA-based monitoring therefore needs no extra sensors, unlike dedicated vibration-based systems that are costly to retrofit \cite{tautzweinert2017}. Yet alarms are used almost entirely \emph{reactively}, and large farms can raise so many that operators struggle to interpret them \cite{qiu2012}. Many alarms are also routine: in our data three alarm codes are present in more than 99\% of 30-day windows, so ``an alarm fired'' is a poor definition of ``something is wrong''. This is why we define the failures to predict from recorded fault time and rotor stops (Section~\ref{sec:prob}) and use alarm counts only as inputs.

	We use the \textbf{Hill of Towie open dataset}, released by RES on behalf of TRIG under CC-BY-4.0 \cite{hot_dataset_v1,hot_dataset_v2}: 21 Siemens SWT-2.3-VS-82 turbines at a commercial wind farm in Scotland, with 10-minute SCADA statistics and the alarm log from January 2016 to April 2026 (version v2.1.0 \tbc{version still to be confirmed with the person who downloaded the data; the brief cited v1.0.0, which ends earlier}). We used five tables: three 10-minute SCADA tables (temperatures, pressures and the main turbine signals), the alarm log and a daily summary that includes the recorded fault time. The data dictionary lists further tables, for active power and for error time, which were not in our files, so we used proxies (Section~\ref{sec:meth}). The temperature tables and the pressure tables for 2019 are missing, so 2019 is not used. \tbc{The number of temperature fields (27 in the earlier draft) and the statement that the publishers list alarm prediction as an intended use, while the released example analyses focus on energy yield and upgrade uplift \cite{res_repo}, still need to be checked against the data dictionary and the record page.}

	\section{Objectives and Problem Statement}\label{sec:prob}

	The \textbf{primary objective} is to predict, using only standard 10-minute SCADA data and the alarm log, whether a turbine will enter a fault or downtime episode in the near future, and to be honest about how much better than chance this is. The four objectives are to:
	\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{O\arabic*}]
		\item build a degradation indicator: a model of normal behaviour whose gap to the observed temperatures is a health score;
		\item predict fault and downtime episodes within 30 days with models of increasing complexity, and compare each with a random score on the false-alarm budget of one per turbine-month;
		\item test robustness: to a turbine unseen in training, to missing sensor groups, and across years;
		\item explain the predictions, as attribution to model inputs and not as proof of cause.
	\end{enumerate}

	\paragraph{Targets.} The alarm log contains many routine events, so alarm codes are not the target. Target A marks a usable day as a \emph{fault day} when the recorded fault time (\texttt{TimeErr} in the daily summary) is at least four hours; target B marks a \emph{stop} when the generator speed is below 200\,rpm for at least 18 consecutive 10-minute intervals (three hours) while the primary wind speed is at least 4\,m/s. In both, fault days or stops separated by fewer than seven fault-free days form one \emph{episode}. The meaning of \texttt{TimeErr} is not documented by the data provider; we infer it from its name and from its agreement with the SCADA stop time (correlation 0.86), so ``fault'' here means ``recorded fault time'' and not ``confirmed component failure''. The two targets are related cross-checks, not independent confirmations: about three quarters of episodes of one have an episode of the other starting within seven days.

	\paragraph{Formal statement.} Let $k \in \{1,\ldots,21\}$ index turbines and $d$ index turbine-days, with $\mathbf{x}_{k,d}$ the features of day $d$ built from data up to the end of that day only. The model input is the \emph{observation window} of the last $W=7$ days, summarised into window means and standard deviations. For the \emph{prediction horizon} $H=30$ days, the label is
	\begin{equation}
		y_{k,d} =
		\begin{cases}
			1 & \text{if an episode starts on turbine } k \text{ in } (d,\, d+H], \\
			0 & \text{otherwise.}
		\end{cases}
	\end{equation}
	Rows that cannot be labelled honestly carry no label and are not used for training or scoring: days inside an episode, days whose next episode starts 30 to 60 days ahead (the \emph{exclusion zone}, because the label there is ambiguous), days whose horizon reaches into another data split, and poorly observed days (Figure~\ref{fig:timeline}). We learn a score $f(\mathbf{x}_{k,d})$ and raise an alert when it exceeds a threshold $\tau$ chosen on the validation years so that the false alarms stay at one per turbine-month.

	\paragraph{Scope and metrics.} Data are split chronologically, since neighbouring days are strongly correlated and a random split would leak the future. Because about half of the labelled days are positive after the exclusions (Table~\ref{tab:labels}), accuracy is uninformative, and so is recall at a fixed number of false alarms: a random score alone reaches episode recall of 0.76 to 0.84 at the budget. We therefore report the area under the precision--recall curve (PR-AUC) against the random-score value, episode recall (the share of episodes with at least one alert 1 to 30 days before the start) next to the random-score recall, the median lead time and the false alarms per turbine-month, with 95\% intervals that resample whole turbines. We do not report precision.

	\section{Related Work}

	\tbc{The citations in this section and in the Background come from the earlier draft and have not been re-checked against the sources; the project rule is to cite only sources that have been verified. Check each statement and each number below against its source before submission.}

	Tautz-Weinert and Watson \cite{tautzweinert2017} group SCADA-based condition monitoring into trending, clustering, normal behaviour modelling, damage modelling, and alarm/expert-system assessment; our work combines the last two by using alarms as inputs to a model of SCADA behaviour and by building a normal-behaviour indicator. Qiu et al.\ \cite{qiu2012} first analysed SCADA alarms directly, showing that alarm volumes overwhelm operators and that alarm sequences carry diagnostic and prognostic information. Leahy et al.\ \cite{leahy2018} trained support vector machines on SCADA data from one Irish turbine, with recall generally above 80\% and fault prediction up to 24\,h ahead. Shah and Tan \cite{shah2025} forecast alarm time series with an LSTM for 14 Senvion MM82 turbines; accuracy fell from 82\% at a 10-minute horizon to 41\% at 30 minutes, which shows the horizon trade-off that we did not examine (Section~\ref{sec:disc}).

	Closest to our normal-behaviour indicator, Barrera and Vidal \cite{barrera2026} detect generator faults from SCADA data alone at an eight-turbine farm with one winding-overheating failure. They model healthy phase temperatures using Gramian angular field images and CNNs, flag faults as deviations, fine-tune a global model per turbine (MAE 2.016 $\rightarrow$ 1.479) and use integrated gradients to explain predictions. Gück et al.\ \cite{gueck2024} released the CARE benchmark (36 turbines, three farms) with a score rewarding earliness and penalising false alarm events, which informs our metrics.

	Unlike these studies (Table~\ref{tab:related}), we (i) use openly licensed data so results are reproducible, (ii) define the target as fault and downtime episodes, with two independent definitions, over 21 turbines and about ten years, and (iii) evaluate every model against a random-score floor, with intervals that resample turbines, and score the test years once.

	\section{Methodology}\label{sec:meth}

	\subsection{Data Pre-Processing}
	We built every table from the raw monthly files (590 CSV files, 2016 to April 2026). The three 10-minute SCADA tables were de-duplicated, sentinel values were masked to missing per table (for example 200 and $-50$ in the temperature tables, where they mean ``no reading''; 200 is a real value for yaw and nacelle position and is not masked), and the tables were joined on turbine and timestamp, giving 10.14 million rows. The timestamp is the end of the interval (UTC), so a day holds the intervals whose start lies in it. Nothing was silently dropped: rows keep flags for outage months (2022-08, 2022-09 and 2025-09 have far less than the full set of intervals), for turbine-years whose wind sensor is suspect (four turbine-years), and for the number of masked values. Active power and error time were not available, so we used generator speed against wind speed instead of a power curve, and \texttt{TimeErr} from the daily summary for target A. Alarm events come from the raw alarm log and not from the processed workbooks, which keep about 0.2\% of the alarms.

	Labels were generated as in Section~\ref{sec:prob}. Table~\ref{tab:labels} gives the number of labelled days, positive days and episodes by target and split; the rest of the days are unlabelled by the rules above.

	\subsection{Feature Extraction}
	Each row is one turbine-day, built from data up to the end of that day. For every sensor we take the daily mean over producing intervals (generator speed at least 1000\,rpm, and at least two hours of them) and the daily maximum, plus the fractions of the day producing, idle and stopped, and wind and rotor-speed summaries. For the 7-day window we add the trailing mean and standard deviation (at least four observed days), the count of each of the 12 documented alarm codes, of each alarm tier and of all alarms in the last 7 days, and the days since and the number of fault days in the last 30 days. The table has 70{,}816 turbine-days; each model starts from about 250 candidate columns. A test recomputes random rows after deleting everything after the end of their day and found no feature that changed. Columns are kept and values are filled using the training years only.

	\subsection{Models}
	All models score every day that has features; only labelled days enter fitting and metrics. We compare:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Floors:} a constant score and a random score. The random score is the reference for every result.
		\item \textbf{B1, B2 (rules):} the recent fault history (fault days in the last 30 days and recency), and the 7-day alarm counts.
		\item \textbf{B3:} a pooled healthy curve of generator speed against wind speed; the score is the deviation from it.
		\item \textbf{R1:} for a set of seven bearing, oil and winding temperatures, the largest absolute z-score of the 7-day mean against the turbine's own healthy history.
		\item \textbf{B4:} class-weighted logistic regression with train-only scaling and imputation.
		\item \textbf{R2 and the normal-behaviour model (O1):} ridge regressions that predict ten temperatures (gearbox oil, generator bearings, main bearing, three stator windings, transformer oil, hydraulic oil and one further drivetrain bearing) from wind speed, generator and main-shaft speed, ambient and nacelle temperature, fitted on healthy training days (7{,}635 days that are healthy for both targets, with turbine 2304515 left out of the fit but still scored; 5{,}718 had every input). The residual is predicted minus observed, divided by the turbine's healthy spread, and is made relative to the median of the turbines observed that day, which removed most of the drift between the training and validation years (mean absolute drift 0.44 to 0.06 standard deviations). The indicator is the largest absolute 7-day smoothed residual; R2 scores each day with it.
		\item \textbf{L1:} class-weighted LightGBM on the B4 columns (with and without the normal-behaviour columns), with the positive weight and the columns chosen on the training years. A grid of six settings (leaves and number of trees) was run on the validation years only and the winners were frozen (target A: 7 leaves, 300 trees; target B: 31 leaves, 300 trees).
	\end{itemize}
	Healthy days are labelled days that are not near an episode (seven days after one ends are excluded), have clean quality flags and lie in the training years.

	\subsection{Evaluation Environment}
	Models are fitted on 2016 to 2018 and 2020, settings and thresholds are chosen on 2021 to 2022, and the test years (2023 to April 2026) are scored once, for the frozen configurations (Table~\ref{tab:split}). The threshold gives one false alarm per turbine-month on the validation years and is carried unchanged to the test years. Intervals come from 1{,}000 resamples of whole turbines with the threshold held fixed; they do not include the uncertainty of choosing the model or the threshold. The project is written in Python (pandas, scikit-learn, LightGBM, SHAP); every setting is in one configuration file, all randomness is seeded from it, and every decision is logged. \tbc{Hardware and run times.}

	\subsection{Explanation and Robustness}
	We explain the frozen LightGBM with SHAP on 4{,}000 validation days and measure how stable the ranking is by refitting without each of the 21 turbines in turn. Robustness is tested on the validation years only: refitting without one turbine and scoring that turbine (leave-one-turbine-out), and setting the features of one sensor group (pressure, drivetrain temperature, power electronics, wind and rotor speed) to missing at scoring time, at the threshold of the intact model.

	\section{Simulation Results}\label{sec:findings}
	\subsection{Key Findings and Significance}

	\paragraph{Validation years.} On the tuning years the per-turbine z-score R1 had the highest PR-AUC for both targets (0.577 for A and 0.588 for B, against random scores of 0.465 and 0.483), and recall-based comparisons with the random score were below it for every model (Table~\ref{tab:valtest}). The supervised models, B4 and LightGBM, were below the random-score value on validation.

	\paragraph{Test years.} Tables~\ref{tab:testa} and~\ref{tab:testb} give the single scored run on the test years. The random-score PR-AUC is 0.450 for target A and 0.506 for target B, and \textbf{the 95\% interval of every model contains it}. The highest point estimates are for LightGBM (0.492 and 0.493 with the normal-behaviour columns for A; 0.556 for B) and for logistic regression on target B (0.563), but their intervals overlap the random-score value. R1 falls from 0.577 and 0.588 on validation to 0.448 and 0.499 on test, so its advantage did not hold. No model reaches the random-score episode recall (0.795 and 0.839) at a comparable false-alarm rate; the models that reach recall of 0.55 to 0.68 (R1 and R2) do so at 2.1 to 2.5 false alarms per turbine-month, about twice the budget, because the validation threshold does not carry over. The median lead times of 17 to 30 days are close to the 21 days of a random score.

	\paragraph{Exploratory follow-up (validation years only; not tested on the test years).} The drift between years and the models' reliance on weather-linked inputs suggested two ideas, which we pre-registered and ran on the validation years only (decision D-064): (i) \emph{fleet-relative} features, in which each sensor-derived feature has the median of the same feature over the turbines observed that day subtracted (so no later day is used), optionally also with the month-of-year mean learned on the training years removed; and (ii) low-capacity models, namely LightGBM with very small trees (three leaves, depth two) and L1-penalised logistic regression. All 42 combinations were run and none was selected (Table~\ref{tab:explval}). Fleet-relative features raised the validation PR-AUC of the logistic models for both targets (for target A, logistic regression 0.447 to 0.483 against a random-score value of 0.465) and of the tree models for target B (LightGBM 0.456 to 0.516, very small trees 0.433 to 0.529, against 0.483), but not of LightGBM for target A (0.460 to 0.452); removing the month-of-year mean as well lowered the scores again in almost every case. Every 95\% interval still contains the random-score value (the highest, 0.529 for target B, has the interval [0.440, 0.618]), and recall at the budget stayed between 0.10 and 0.32 against 0.76 to 0.78 for a random score. The year-shift check on training years (Table~\ref{tab:explshift}) puts every model with the raw features above the prevalence of 2020 (0.412 for A, 0.399 for B), and the fleet-relative features give no consistent gain there. Because 42 configurations were compared on two validation years, the best ones are optimistically biased, and validation did not predict the test results for the z-score model (Table~\ref{tab:valtest}), so these are leads for a new pre-registered study with fresh data, not results; none was scored on the test years.

	\subsection{Issues Faced} \label{sec:issue}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Missing tables.} Active power and error time were not in our files, so the power-curve baseline uses rotor speed against wind speed, and the meaning of \texttt{TimeErr} is inferred. We addressed this by defining a second, independent target from the 10-minute data and reporting both.
		\item \textbf{Routine alarms.} Some alarm codes are present in nearly every window, so alarms cannot be the target. We used recorded fault time and rotor stops instead and kept alarm counts as inputs.
		\item \textbf{Almost half of the days are positive.} With a 30-day horizon a random score already reaches recall of 0.76 to 0.84 at the budget. We report PR-AUC and recall against the random-score floor for every model, and we did not change the horizon after seeing results.
		\item \textbf{Drift between years.} Sensor levels and the normal-behaviour residuals shift between the training and validation years, and models with strong fits in training (PR-AUC 0.94 in-sample for LightGBM) lose it on later years. We made the residuals relative to the fleet and used only the training years for fitting.
		\item \textbf{Validation did not predict the test.} The model ranking changed between validation and test, and the threshold did not transfer. We scored the test years once and report both.
		\item \textbf{Unusual turbine.} Turbine 2304515 is positive on 97\% of its validation days; we report results with and without it on validation.
		\item \textbf{Gaps in the data.} Missing 2019 tables, three outage months and four turbine-years with a suspect wind sensor are flagged, not dropped.
	\end{itemize}

	\section{Discussion: Recommendations, Stakeholders, Impact and Ethics}\label{sec:disc}

	\subsection{What the Findings Mean}
	With these data and labels, none of the models shows a gain over a random score on the test years that survives the uncertainty of the estimate, so the models cannot yet tell an operator which turbine to inspect first. This is a limit of the evidence, not proof that prediction is impossible: the target is broad (a 30-day window with about half of days positive), the labels are inferred episodes and not confirmed failures, and the sensors drift between years. The value of any warning still depends on whether its lead time can be acted on: days allow a technician visit and parts to be scheduled during low wind, when lost production is smallest.

	\subsection{Recommendations}
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \textbf{Do not deploy these models for operations.} None beats the random score on the test years; keep existing protection and inspection routines.
		\item \textbf{If a model is used later, use it as decision support, not control,} to rank turbines for human review, with turbine protection as the final safeguard.
		\item \textbf{Set the threshold from costs, on recent data.} Choose $\tau$ to minimise $C_{\text{FP}}N_{\text{FP}} + C_{\text{FN}}N_{\text{FN}}$ (unnecessary inspection vs.\ unplanned stop). Our validation threshold did not carry to later years, so it should be re-chosen on recent data and monitored. \tbc{State the false-alarm and miss costs; none were available to us.}
		\item \textbf{Retrain and recheck after upgrades and across years.} The data period includes the AeroUp and TuneUp upgrades \cite{hot_dataset_v1}; we could not test their effect, and sensor drift across years was visible, so models should be recalibrated and monitored for drift. \tbc{Check the upgrade names and dates against the dataset.}
		\item \textbf{Track the false-warning rate as a key performance indicator,} since frequent false warnings recreate the alarm overload of \cite{qiu2012}.
		\item \textbf{Obtain better labels first:} confirmed failure and maintenance records, active power and the documented error time, which would replace the inferred episodes.
	\end{enumerate}

	As O\&M is a large share of wind LCOE \cite{irena2012}, even modest cuts in unplanned downtime would lower energy costs, while planned maintenance reduces time spent working at height under pressure or in poor weather (Table~\ref{tab:stake}). Our results show that this benefit is not yet established.

	\subsection{Ethical Considerations}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Over-reliance:} the gravest risk is a false negative that a trusting operator fails to catch. The models must supplement, not replace, existing protection and inspections, and our results show that they do not yet beat chance.
		\item \textbf{Alert fatigue:} false positives waste technician time and erode trust, so the threshold is an ethical choice to be set transparently with those who act on it. On the test years our validation thresholds gave between 0.25 and 2.5 false alarms per turbine-month.
		\item \textbf{Accountability and honest reporting:} we report a weak result as weak, with intervals, and we explain predictions as model attribution and not as causes, so that engineers can see why a warning fired and override it.
		\item \textbf{Generalisability:} trained on one farm, turbine type and climate, the model may fail silently elsewhere and must be revalidated before transfer.
		\item \textbf{Data use:} the CC-BY-4.0 licence requires attribution to TRIG and RES. \tbc{That the publishers removed SCADA user identifiers \cite{hot_dataset_v1} and the exact citation text still need to be checked against the record page.} Commercial use on proprietary data would raise data-ownership questions.
		\item \textbf{Limits of the open data:} there are no measured power, confirmed failure or maintenance records, and alarm descriptions exist for only 12 codes. ``Fault'' and ``degradation'' are inferred. \tbc{Ethics review (Cyrus): confirm and extend this section.}
	\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Future Work}
	Obtain confirmed failure and maintenance records and the missing power and error-time tables, and redefine the targets from them; evaluate shorter horizons and episode-onset-only targets as a separate, pre-registered study (we did not examine the horizon); re-estimate the alert threshold on recent data and test how stable it is; study the fleet-relative features and low-capacity models of the exploratory runs on fresh data with a pre-registered protocol; add the upgrade analysis once the installation dates are available; validate on other datasets (e.g.\ CARE \cite{gueck2024}); exploit the $\sim$1\,Hz data added in v2.1.0 \cite{hot_dataset_v2} with sequence models; and run a shadow trial logging predictions alongside live operations to measure real lead time.

	\section{Conclusions}
	We asked whether routine SCADA data can warn of a fault or long stoppage in a wind turbine 30 days ahead. On 21 turbines and about ten years of data, with two independent fault definitions, the answer from this work is not yet. Three findings stand out:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \textbf{No model beats the random score on the test years.} Every 95\% interval for PR-AUC contains the random-score value (0.450 for A, 0.506 for B); the best point estimates are 0.493 (LightGBM, A) and 0.563 (logistic regression, B).
		\item \textbf{The validation ranking and threshold did not carry over.} The z-score model that led on validation (0.577 and 0.588) fell to the random level on test (0.448 and 0.499), and its alerts reached about 2.1 false alarms per turbine-month against a budget of one.
		\item \textbf{The normal-behaviour indicator and the explanations are stable but weak.} The indicator drifts between years unless made relative to the fleet and is only slightly higher before episodes; the model's ranking of inputs is stable across turbines but describes a model without demonstrated skill.
	\end{enumerate}
	Exploratory runs with fleet-relative features, on validation years only, raised some scores but not beyond the margin of error and were not tested on the test years; they are leads for future work. Of the objectives, O1 (indicator) and O4 (explanation) were built and found weak, O2 (prediction) was built and evaluated honestly but does not beat chance, and O3 (robustness) was met for unseen turbines and missing sensor groups on the validation years, while the upgrade analysis was not run and the year-shift check was done only on training years as part of the exploratory runs. Our key recommendation is to not deploy these models and to improve the labels and the evaluation (confirmed failures, power and error-time data, recent-data thresholds) before repeating the study; the first next step is to obtain those records.
